\title{How Short Can a Branchless Sort Be?\\ Certified Optimality of AlphaDev's sort3 and sort4 Kernels,\\ and What Stands
in the Way for sort5}
\author{Zhiyang Chen}
\date{October 2026}
\begin{document}\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
AlphaDev (Nature 2023) found x86 kernels that sort 3, 4 and 5 integers in 17, 28 and 42 instructions. We ask whether
shorter kernels exist. We state a precise model --- the instructions \texttt{mov}, \texttt{cmp}, \texttt{cmovl},
\texttt{cmovg}, \texttt{cmovle}, \texttt{cmovge} on signed 32-bit values, inputs and outputs in memory, every instruction
counted, unbounded registers, correctness for all inputs (with ties) and all initial register and flag values --- and reduce
the question to a finite search through a chain of lemmas, each with a paper proof and a Lean~4 proof. Results:
(i) sort3 needs 17 instructions. We give ten independent proofs, among them LRAT certificates checked by the verified checker
\texttt{cake\_lpr}, a Lean theorem that uses only the Lean kernel and the three standard axioms, and a Rocq proof.
(ii) sort4 needs 28 instructions, by an exhaustive search over $1.39\times10^9$ abstract states with reviewed pruning rules;
its 123\,GB certificate is checked in Lean up to the bound $k \le 17$.
(iii) For sort5 we find no kernel shorter than 42. We find a second kernel family with 8 instead of 9 comparisons, show that
no pure min/max kernel of length $\le 41$ exists and that garbage registers never help, and isolate the remaining open case.
(iv) The model matters: if \texttt{cmp} and \texttt{cmov} may read memory (legal on x86, excluded by AlphaDev), the kernels fit
in 7, 15, 26 and 39 instructions for $n = 2, \dots, 5$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Mankowitz et al.~\cite{alphadev} used deep reinforcement learning to find short branchless sorting kernels for 3, 4 and 5
elements, of length 17, 28 and 42 (the human-written sorting networks need 18, 28 and 46). The kernels are now in LLVM libc++.
AlphaDev reports a brute-force argument that no sort3 kernel is shorter than 17 (about $10^{32}$ programs, with pruning), but
gives no certificate and no exact model, and makes no claim for sort4 and sort5. Ullrich and Hack~\cite{ullrich} enumerate
sorting kernels in a register-only model and report optimal lengths 11 ($n=3$) and 20 ($n=4$); their model has one scratch
register, only \texttt{cmovl}/\texttt{cmovg}, distinct inputs only, no reads of uninitialised registers, and outputs fixed in
the input registers, and there is no certificate. A lower bound in that model does not transfer to AlphaDev's setting
(Section~\ref{sec:model}). No lower bound for $n=5$ was known.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1) A precise model of AlphaDev's setting, including memory and the exact counting (Section~\ref{sec:model}).
(2) A chain of soundness lemmas from x86 semantics to a finite search problem (Section~\ref{sec:chain}); one of them, the
\emph{snapshot lemma}, shows that the natural register model (``outputs in the input registers at the end'') is too strict
and gives the exact replacement.
(3) Two SAT encodings with CEGIS and symmetry breaking, two enumerators, and verified checkers in Lean and Rocq
(Section~\ref{sec:search}).
(4) sort3 = 17 with ten independent proofs, including a kernel-only Lean theorem; sort4 = 28 by exhaustive search
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).
(5) A large discovery effort for sort5 with exact scopes, two new structural lemmas and the remaining open case
(Section~\ref{sec:sort5}).
(6) Side results: shorter kernels with memory operands, and kernels with a shorter critical path at AlphaDev's length.

\paragraph{Main results.}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
In MM with $I_1$ and unbounded registers, no program of length $\le L-1$ sorts all $x \in \mathbb{Z}_{32}^n$ (for all garbage
and flags), for $(n, L) = (3, 17)$ (certified: \texttt{cake\_lpr}, Lean, Rocq) and $(4, 28)$ (exhaustive search; certificate
checked in Lean up to $k \le 17$). Hence AlphaDev's sort3 and sort4 kernels are shortest.
\end{theorem}
For $n = 5$ the question stays open; Section~\ref{sec:sort5} gives the evidence and the exact remaining case.
Evidence that RF differs in practice: every AlphaDev kernel, with loads and stores removed, ends with its outputs in other
registers (e.g.\ sort3 in $P_0, S_{11}, P_1$), so it is not an RF program of the same length.

The most delicate link is the snapshot lemma (Lemma~\ref{lem:snapshot}, Appendix L5). Its proof needs neither ``loads
first, stores last'' nor AlphaDev's read-once/write-once rule: it handles reloads, spills and stores in the middle of the
code, by one fresh register per store segment and by renaming the live range of the last load of each segment.

\section{Search}\label{sec:search}
\paragraph{Abstraction.} By L1 a program is correct iff it is correct on one representative of each weak order type
(13/75/541). For lower bounds any subset is enough (e.g.\ the $n!$ permutations, or a CEGIS set) --- but permutations with
$I_0$ are \emph{not} a relaxation of weak orders with $I_1$ (a \texttt{cmp} of two copies of one input gives eq).
\paragraph{Relaxation.} Scratch registers start as $T > $ all inputs, flags start eq (L2). This is sound; it can only make
the relaxed optimum smaller (a $T$ register is a known $+\infty$).
\paragraph{SAT encodings.} \texttt{sat}: one-hot operands, CEGIS, NOP suffix, rules canon, pcanon, comm, dead, flags, cmpord, movfresh,
snapcmov, mincmp with a rewriting (normal-form) proof. \texttt{sat2}: binary operands, sweep, rules with a lex-least proof.
\paragraph{Enumeration.} \texttt{enum}: BFS over sets of distinct ``columns'' (Lemma S: with unbounded registers and snapshot
outputs, a \texttt{mov} is only needed as half of a copy), exact deduplication, admissible heuristics, and the rule NG
(normal form for strict programs; reviewed). \texttt{critic}: an independent register-level IDDFS.
\paragraph{Verified checkers.} lean-reflect and Rocq check the enumerator's closure certificates (every successor of a kept
state is kept or pruned by a proved rule, and no kept state is sorted); lean-lrat checks LRAT proofs of CNFs produced by an
encoder that is proved correct in Lean.
\paragraph{Analytic bounds.} $\#\texttt{cmp} \ge \lceil\log_2 n!\rceil$ (3/5/7; tight for $n=5$ with a 7-\texttt{cmp} kernel,
so the sorting-network bound 9 does \emph{not} hold); $\#\texttt{cmov} \ge$ Huffman$(n)$ = 5/8/12 (Kraft inequality on the
paths of each output); for $n=3$, $\#\texttt{cmov} \ge 6$, and for $n=4$, $\#\texttt{cmov} \ge 10$, by exhaustive search in a
relaxation.

\subsection{The sort3 bound: independent proofs and their trust bases}\label{sec:sort3trust}
\begin{theorem}[sort3]\label{thm:sort3}
In AlphaDev's model $M_{AD}(3, I_1)$ --- three signed 32-bit inputs in memory cells, sorted in place; instructions
\texttt{mov} (load, store, register move), \texttt{cmp}, \texttt{cmovl}, \texttt{cmovg}, \texttt{cmovle}, \texttt{cmovge}; \texttt{cmp}
and \texttt{cmov} on registers only (MM-1); unbounded registers; correct for every input, every initial register value and
every initial flag state; every instruction counts --- no program of length $\le 16$ sorts. AlphaDev's 17-instruction kernel is
therefore optimal. Formal statements (Lean): \texttt{LeanReflect.sort3\_mm\_lb :
$\forall$ q, MMProg 3 q $\to$ q.length $\le$ 16 $\to$ $\lnot$ XCorrect 3 q}, and the kernel-only
\texttt{KProto.D3k10I1G.lower\_bound\_kernel\_x86} for the register core.
\end{theorem}
Every route below proves ``no RS program with $k \le 10$'' ($n=3$, $I_1$); the step to $M_{AD}$ ($L \le 16$) is
Lemma~\ref{lem:snapshot} (Lean: \texttt{l5}, \texttt{mm\_lower\_bound}, kernel only).

\subsection{The sort4 bound}
\begin{theorem}[sort4]\label{thm:sort4}
In $M_{AD}(4, I_1)$ with unbounded registers no program of length $\le 27$ sorts; AlphaDev's 28-instruction kernel is optimal.
The same holds in $I_0$ (AlphaDev's sort4 kernel also sorts after replacing \texttt{cmovle}/\texttt{cmovge} by
\texttt{cmovl}/\texttt{cmovg}).
\end{theorem}
\emph{Evidence.} The enumerator explores all strict-correct register cores with $k \le 19$ on the 24 permutations
($1.39\times10^9$ states, 96 minutes on 40 cores, peak 168\,GB) and finds none. Its pruning rules, including the strict normal
form NG, have paper proofs reviewed by two reviewers and by an independent model (Codex), and NG is also proved in Lean
(\texttt{NGMain.lower\_bound\_strict}, standard axioms). The admissible heuristic was tested along every prefix of all 15
AlphaDev register cores (0 violations). The closure certificate (123\,GB, 16.7\,GB compressed) is checked by the verified Lean
checker up to $k \le 17$ (\texttt{sort4\_x\_lb17}, 2.56 million states) and by the Rocq checker up to $k \le 14$; checking the
full $k \le 19$ certificate needs a faster verified checker (about 12--40 hours estimated) and is left as future work.
Independent partial results agree: \texttt{sat2} ($k \le 12$, SAT) and the bounds tool (case $c = 9$, exact set model).

\subsection{Latency: AlphaDev's kernels are not Pareto-optimal}\label{sec:latency}
Depth model: critical path over read-after-write edges on registers and flags (register renaming, so write-after-read and
write-after-write do not count); \texttt{cmov} always reads its destination, its source and the flags; latencies from
uops.info in three models: U (all 1), E (\texttt{mov} 0), Z2 (Zen~2: \texttt{mov} 0, \texttt{cmp}$\to$\texttt{cmov} 2).
Lower bounds use a \emph{relaxed DAG}: each \texttt{cmp} a flag node, each \texttt{cmov} a select node, \texttt{mov} free;
its depth is at most the program's depth, for any length and any output rule (proofs reviewed). Results (DRAT-checked UNSAT
for the bounds, judge-checked programs for the constructions):
\begin{itemize}
\item minimum depth: $n=3$: 4 (U, E), 5 (Z2); $n=4$: 5 (U, E), 6 (Z2); $n=5$: 5 or 6 (U, E; a depth-6 kernel with $k = 174$ exists, and depth $\ge 5$ is DRAT-checked);
\item $n=3$: every program of depth $\le 4$ needs $k \ge 12$; a $k=11$ program of depth 5 exists, so AlphaDev's sort3
$(k, \text{depth}) = (11, 6)$ is dominated;
\item $n=4$: a $k = 20$ kernel exists at depth 5;
\item $n=5$, $k=32$: a kernel of depth U10/E10 beats AlphaDev's Sort5\_42 (U13/E11/Z2 16) at the same length.
\end{itemize}
AlphaDev optimised length for the fixed sorts (it reports that length and latency correlate); our results show that at
optimal length, depth can still be improved.

\section{sort5: the search for a shorter kernel}\label{sec:sort5}
The target is a strict-correct register core with $k \le 31$, i.e.\ an MM kernel of length $\le 41$. A full lower-bound
proof by the methods of Section~\ref{sec:results} is out of reach: extrapolation from $n = 4$ gives at least $10^{15}$
abstract states. We therefore ran a large discovery effort (stochastic search, exact routing for fixed value skeletons,
exhaustive searches in restricted program classes, LLM-guided program design) and recorded every negative result with its
exact scope. Write a register core's cost as $k = c + g + v$ (\texttt{cmp}s, \texttt{cmov}s, \texttt{mov}s).

\paragraph{The known optima.} We collected 274{,}035 correct programs with $k \le 32$; all have $k = 32$ exactly and fall into
30 value-set classes. 27 classes have $c = 9$ and $g + v = 23$ (AlphaDev's split is $9 + 18 + 5$; we also found $9 + 21 + 2$);
3 classes are new and have $c = 8$ and $g + v = 24$ ($8 + 20 + 4$, $8 + 22 + 2$). So sort5 at AlphaDev's length is possible
with 8 comparisons, and in every class we found, fewer comparisons cost exactly as many extra \texttt{cmov}s and copies.
One $c = 9$ class uses values that are not the term set of any 9-comparator sorting network.

\paragraph{Two lemmas.}
\begin{proposition}[garbage never helps]\label{prop:garbage}
Every strict-correct program can be transformed into a program that never reads a register before writing it, with no more
instructions and no more \texttt{cmp}s.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proposition}[no lattice kernel]\label{prop:lattice}
No sort5 register core with $k \le 31$ exists in which every register always holds a min/max term of the inputs.
\end{proposition}
Proposition~\ref{prop:garbage} has a reviewed proof sketch and was tested on 5{,}606 programs (0 failures).
Proposition~\ref{prop:lattice} is an exhaustive breadth-first search ($1.51\times10^9$ states, unbounded registers, reviewed
tool, 9/9 positive controls on known $k = 32$ kernels). Together they show: a 41-instruction sort5 kernel, if it exists, must
write at least one value that is not a min/max of the inputs (for example a ``partner'' carried along after a comparison).

\paragraph{Exclusions within stated scopes.} No register core with $k \le 31$ exists
\begin{itemize}
\item for any value skeleton of the 16 flag classes of the known optima with min/max operands (640 skeletons each), of the
177 nine- and 4{,}609 ten-comparator sorting networks, of 1{,}684 ``recompute instead of copy'' skeletons, of all 161 $c = 8$
skeletons of length 32, and of more than 30{,}000 further neighbour and non-lattice skeletons (exact routing, no register cap);
\item using only the terms of any 9- or 10-comparator network, or of a known optimum plus one or two extra values
(about 68{,}000 term pools, exact search);
\item of the form ``sort four inputs, then insert the fifth'' (inserting needs $\ge 12$ instructions, DRAT-checked);
\item with $c \le 8$ and all \texttt{cmp} operands min/max terms (exhaustive);
\item within three to four changes of either of the two best 41-instruction near misses (wrong on 9 and 14 of the 541 input
orders); no window of 4--7 instructions of these, of the first $c = 8$ kernel (up to 8), or of 17 further $c = 8$ kernels (4--6)
can be replaced by a shorter one.
\end{itemize}
Stochastic search ($\approx 7\times10^9$ proposals), quality-diversity search and LLM-guided design found many 32-instruction
cores and no 31.

\paragraph{The open case.} What remains are garbage-free programs that write one or more non-min/max values. For one or two
such values we built SAT encodings (reviewed as sound), but they decide none of the 1{,}929 cases within an hour, and an exact
enumeration would need $10^{11}$--$10^{12}$ states; programs with three or more such values were not searched exhaustively.
We therefore conjecture, but do not claim, that 42 is optimal for sort5.

\section{Threats to validity: what is outside the model}\label{sec:threats}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Memory operands (MM+).} x86 allows \texttt{cmp r,m}, \texttt{cmp m,r}, \texttt{cmovcc r,m}. With them, sort3, sort4
and sort5 fit in 15, 26 and 39 instructions (Section~\ref{sec:beyond}). So AlphaDev's kernels are optimal only under MM-1. Our
snapshot lemma does not hold for MM+ (Lemma L8: only $L \ge k_{\mathrm{RS}}$); an MM+ optimum needs its own search.
\item \textbf{Other instructions}: SIMD min/max, branches, arithmetic and masks, \texttt{setcc}, \texttt{lea}, unsigned
conditions (they read flags that are not a function of the signed order). Adding \texttt{cmove}, \texttt{cmovne} and
\texttt{xchg} gave no shorter kernel in our searches, and \texttt{xchg} is provably redundant (Lemma L11).
\item \textbf{Other costs}: latency, throughput, code size (AlphaDev reports that length and latency correlate for fixed sorts).
\item \textbf{Data}: signed int32. Unsigned or floating-point kernels use other opcodes.
\item \textbf{Registers}: our lower bounds allow unbounded registers (stronger); real x86 has at most 14 free GPRs.
\item \textbf{Trust base}: the Lean kernel (and, for some routes, the Lean compiler or the Rocq \texttt{native\_\-compute}
feature),
\texttt{cake\_lpr}, the Python encoders for the \texttt{cake\_lpr} routes, and the agreement of the x86 semantics with real
hardware and the ACL2 x86isa model. For sort4 the trusted base also includes the enumerator for $18 \le k \le 19$.
\item \textbf{Hardware}: all runs used one AMD EPYC 9654; no Intel processor was tested.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
In AlphaDev's own model, the 17-instruction sort3 kernel is optimal, with machine-checked certificates down to a kernel-only Lean
theorem, and the 28-instruction sort4 kernel is optimal by exhaustive search. For sort5 we found no kernel shorter than 42, a new
8-comparison family of the same length, and two structural results that reduce the open question to kernels that carry
non-min/max values. Closing that case needs a new idea, for example a lemma that bounds the number of such values in a
shortest kernel. Two further observations matter for the original claim: AlphaDev's lengths are optimal only because its rules
forbid memory operands, and at optimal length the critical path of its kernels can still be shortened.
